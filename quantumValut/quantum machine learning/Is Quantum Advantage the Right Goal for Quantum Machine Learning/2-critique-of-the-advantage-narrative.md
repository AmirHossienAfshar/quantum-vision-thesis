# A Critical Look at "Quantum Advantage"

## The seven common ways QML papers claim "beating" classical ML
1. Asymptotic run time of a training algorithm
2. Whether a learning problem is efficiently solvable for a given data
   distribution
3. Expressivity of the model class
4. Number of samples needed to learn
5. Average- or worst-case generalization error
6. Structure of the optimization landscape (trainability)
7. Test error on a small-scale practical benchmark

> [!note] Publication bias
> The authors flag a strong positive-results bias — headline phrases like
> "rigorous quantum speed-up," "exponentially fewer experiments," or large
> % improvements over GANs are common. Negative/no-advantage results also
> exist (e.g. in trainability and sample-complexity research) but get less
> spotlight.

## Why the current toolbox can't answer the advantage question well

### 1. Artificial, hand-picked settings
Provable exponential speed-ups tend to come from problems engineered to favor quantum structure (biased data distributions / ground truths). This tells us little about real applications — and clashes with ML's whole ethos of being applied *agnostically* to problems whose structure isn't known in advance.

### 2. Extreme data-loading assumptions
Early "traditional" QML work (quantum matrix inversion, quantum SVMs, etc.) chasing speed-ups over algorithms like SVMs or neural nets usually requires strong, often unrealistic assumptions about how data is loaded into and read out of the quantum computer

### 3. Average/worst-case claims over huge model families don't decide individual models
Many advantage *and* no-advantage proofs are about very large families of models. Proving something about the whole family doesn't rule out one *specific* sub-model behaving completely differently — the same way average properties of "all classical models" wouldn't rule out something like boosting or GANs performing exceptionally.

### 4. objective benchmark studies are hard to trust
- Comparisons are usually against one narrow classical baseline on small datasets.
- Small architecture tweaks can swing results significantly.
- Very few studies attempt reproduction or interrogate whether the *metrics* used are even the right ones.
- We know almost nothing about how small-scale empirical results will scale
  up to realistic problem sizes.

### 5. Skepticism about pivoting to "quantum data"
As classical-data QML struggles to show commercial value, a common move is: "the real future is analyzing *quantum* data" (data generated by quantum processes, rather than classical datasets). The authors' caution: this shift might be less about finding a mature use case and more about subconsciously chasing a problem setting where our existing (weak) proof techniques happen
to work better.
